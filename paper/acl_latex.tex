\begin{abstract}
  Distillation of reasoning chains from Large Language Models (LLMs) into their smaller siblings is a standard training approach.
  Frequently, researchers do not have full access to a teacher model and its internal representations, which limits the resulting performance.
  We propose a novel blueprint for efficient offline distillation that recursively uses lightweight epistemic and aleatoric complexity approximations for selective distillation.
  Specifically, for three open models (3-4B), at each epoch, we estimate epistemic uncertainty using a medium model (70-72B) as an aleatoric proxy and sample only a small subset of the reasoning chains for training.
  Our approach consistently outperforms naive distillation with comparable computational budgets ($57.15 \pm 1.01$ vs 51.61 on average) and offers a much steeper learning curve.
  Additionally, we present a comparative analysis of methods to generate and normalize distillation traces.
  We publish our code and data\footnote{TODO}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Modern LLMs come in different sizes, and their performance is often correlated with the number of parameters.
The largest of them demonstrate exceptional results and span 1-2 trillion parameters.
Further domain adaptation \citep{ICLR2024_d51cd79a,lu2024finetuninglargelanguagemodels} or even inference at this scale requires massive computational resources \citep{wan2024efficientlargelanguagemodels} unavailable to most practitioners.
It prompts the need for capable smaller models \citep{chen2026rolesmallmodelsllm} that can be fine-tuned and deployed in resource-constrained environments.

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{img/recursive_caft_scheme.png}
  \caption{Recursive offline distillation scheme}
  \label{fig:recursive_caft_scheme}
\end{figure}

A prominent approach for small model training is distillation from a larger teacher model \citep{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork, sanh2020distilbertdistilledversionbert, xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge}.
Many methods rely on having access to the online teacher \citep{ICLR2024_5be69a58, jiao-etal-2020-tinybert, ICLR2024_8ac015d4}, which is not always possible \citep{zhou-etal-2023-bridging, ryskulov2026efficientknowledgedistillationllms}.
It forces practitioners to explore offline distillation, where the student model is trained on the teacher's pre-generated text output \citep{li-etal-2023-symbolic, hsieh-etal-2023-distilling}.

We propose a fully automated recursive pipeline for offline distillation (Figure \ref{fig:recursive_caft_scheme}, described in \ref{sec:method}) based on uncertainty decomposition \citep{wang2025aleatoricepistemicexploringuncertainty}. The method consistently outperforms naive distillation ($57.15 \pm 1.01$ vs 51.61 on average), and it is designed to work in resource-constrained environments.

Additionally, we provide a comparative analysis of augmentation strategies for synthetic reasoning chains when the teacher model does not perform perfectly in the target distillation domain and conduct sensitivity studies of the synthetic trace generation and normalization methods and sampling strategies.

\section{Related Work}

Recent work improved distillation through training objectives \citep{pmlr-v235-ko24c,xu-etal-2026-harnessing}, data selection \citep{pmlr-v306-just26a,li2025naturalthoughtsselectingdistillingreasoning,yang-etal-2026-select2reason}, and curriculum learning \citep{jiang-etal-2025-teach,zhang-liu-2026-calibrated}.
Joint supervision with rationales and labels improves data efficiency \citep{hsieh-etal-2023-distilling}.
Curriculum benefits vary with model capability, task complexity, and the choice of difficulty metric \citep{jia-etal-2026-makes}.
ELAD \citep{zhang-etal-2024-elad} selects uncertain student explanations for teacher revision.
Complexity-aware fine-tuning \citep{goncharov2026complexityawarefinetuning} selects reasoning examples by single-token entropy.
We recursively sample pre-generated traces using student-proxy entropy differences.

Trace synthesis includes explaining known solutions \citep{li2024distillingalgorithmicreasoning,wang2025reverseengineeredreasoningopenendedgeneration} and learning from corrections \citep{an2024learningmistakesmakesllm, ICLR2025_0967d7c8}.
We compare direct generation and various augmentations on the same task under constrained trace lengths.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Our central hypothesis} is that we can approximate epistemic uncertainty of the student model with its entropy and entropy of a larger LLM.
\citet{goncharov2026complexityawarefinetuning} show that a single-token entropy (Figure \ref{fig:single_token_entropy_illustration}) is a reasonable complexity predictor for a given entry in a question-answering dataset.
We assume that it approximates the total uncertainty and that we can decompose it into epistemic and aleatoric components \citep{pmlr-v80-depeweg18a, malinin2021uncertaintyestimationautoregressivestructured}.
With scaling laws \citep{kaplan2020scalinglawsneurallanguage} in mind, we hypothesize that larger models should have lower total uncertainty.
Therefore, in comparison to a small student model (3-4B), its substantially larger sibling (70-72B\footnote{This particular size is chosen based on the imposed resource constraints (80GB VRAM) and open model availability}) could be treated as a weak aleatoric proxy.

For a question $x$, assume $H(x)=A(x)+E(x)$, where $H_S$, $H_P$ - single-token entropies (student and proxy), $A$ - shared aleatoric component, and $E_S$, $E_P$ - model-specific epistemic components.
If its residual epistemic uncertainty satisfies $E_P(x)\leq\varepsilon(x)$, then
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    A(x) &= H_P(x)-E_P(x) \geq H_P(x) - \varepsilon(x) \\
    E_S(x) &= H_S(x) - A(x) \leq H_S(x) - H_P(x) + \varepsilon(x)
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:aleatoric_proxy_error}
\end{equation}
Thus, the entropy difference (\emph{entropy gain}) approximates the student's epistemic uncertainty with error at most $\varepsilon(x)$.

\begin{algorithm}
  \caption{Recursive offline distillation via epistemic complexity approximation}
  \label{alg:recursive_distillation}
  \small
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require Student $S$, proxy model $P$, epochs $T$
    \Require Questions $D$, ground-truth answers $y_x$, and precomputed reasoning traces $r_x$
    \Ensure Trained student $S$
    \ForAll{$x\in D$}
    \State $H_P(x)\gets\Call{Entropy}{P,x}$
    \EndFor
    \For{$t=1,\ldots,T$}
    \ForAll{$x\in D$}
    \State $H_S(x)\gets\Call{Entropy}{S,x}$
    \State $\mathrm{EG}(x)\gets\max\{H_S(x)-H_P(x),0\}$
    \EndFor
    \State $Q\gets\Call{WeightedSample}{D,\mathrm{EG},1024}$
    \State $Q_A\gets\text{first }256\text{ questions in }Q$
    \State $B_R\gets[(x,r_x):x\in Q]$
    \State $B_A\gets[(x,y_x):x\in Q_A]$
    \State $S\gets\Call{Train}{S,B_R\mathbin{\Vert}B_A}$
    \EndFor
    \State \Return $S$
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{The proposed pipeline} consists of the following steps (Figure \ref{fig:recursive_caft_scheme}, Algorithm \ref{alg:recursive_distillation}):
(1) prompt the larger model to answer all questions in the dataset with a single token once (or an ensemble, averaging entropies), collect entropy, assume it is an aleatoric uncertainty proxy;
(2) for each training epoch,
(2.1) prompt the student model to answer all questions in the dataset with a single token, collect entropy;
(2.2) calculate entropy gain $EG(x) = \max\{H_S(x)-H_P(x),0\}$;
(2.3.1) sample 1024 synthetic reasoning chains without replacement, weighted by positive entropy gain;
(2.3.2) sample 256 single-token answers similarly, as a subset of the previous sample;
(2.4) concatenate samples;
(2.5) train the student using LoRA \citep{hu2021loralowrankadaptationlarge} on reasoning traces first, followed by single-token responses.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Dataset:} Multiple-choice question-answering dataset MMLU Pro \citep{wang2024mmluprorobustchallengingmultitask}.
It has questions of different complexities from 14 domains: ten options each, one correct option. Random train/test split - 80/20.

\paragraph{LLMs:}
Aleatoric proxy: ensemble of two medium models\footnote{All models and datasets are published under permissive licenses that allow them to be used for research purposes} (averaging their entropies) - LLaMA 3.3 70B \citep{grattafiori2024llama3herdmodels}, Qwen 2.5 72B \citep{qwen2.5}.
Students: Qwen2.5 3B \citep{qwen2.5}, Phi-4-Mini \citep{microsoft2025phi4minitechnicalreportcompact}, LLaMA 3.2 3B \citep{grattafiori2024llama3herdmodels}.
Reasoning trace source: DeepSeek V4 Pro and Flash \citep{deepseekai2026deepseekv4highlyefficientmilliontoken} via API.

\paragraph{Hardware:} We run all experiments\footnote{Training hyperparameters can be found in Appendix~\ref{sec:training_hyperparameters}.} on a single A100/H100\footnote{We had to switch cards depending on availability} GPU with 80GB of VRAM to validate the method in resource-constrained environments.

\subsubsection{Our method and results}

We evaluate the proposed pipeline for 200 epochs (205k questions seen) and 2 thinking budgets (2048 and 4096 tokens).
It is compared under similar data budgets with:\\
\emph{Direct reasoning trace, Corrected answer} - naive distillation on the most promising types of reasoning traces (see \ref{sec:synth_trace_analysis});\\
\emph{Student entropy (deterministic), Entropy gain (deterministic)} - alternative pipelines with weighted sampling replaced with greedy sampling;\\
\emph{Student entropy (sampled once, k={x})} - distillation executed against k samples selected once by student entropy (not recursively at each epoch).

\emph{Entropy gain (sampled), Student entropy (deterministic), Entropy gain (deterministic)} use the proposed data mix (reasoning traces/single-token answers = 1024/256); other methods use only reasoning traces.

Notably, the proposed data mix increases the number of training tokens by only 0.003\% - 256 extra tokens compared to 8,388,608 tokens in 1024 reasoning chains.

All randomly sampled methods use 3 random seeds, reporting $\pm$ 95\% confidence intervals, and deterministic methods are evaluated once with greedy sampling.

\subsection{Main Results}

Table \ref{tab:distillation_main_results} and Figure \ref{fig:main_results_ghraphs} show the results for MMLU Pro.
We see that the proposed method produces a significant improvement over naive distillation (up to $10\%$ gain).
For both reasoning budgets, it provides the best result on average across three models in comparison with other approaches.
Greedy sampling demonstrates subpar and less stable curves, though surpassing the baselines.
Greedy distillation based on student entropy sampled once \citep{goncharov2026complexityawarefinetuning} shows signs of severe degradation after the initial uplift.
However, Qwen 2.5 3B, notably, demonstrates the best performance with it at k=3072, but degrades at smaller sample sizes, revealing the fragility of the approach.

We also note that our method helps with the model calibration, significantly enhancing ROC AUC of single-token entropy for the first half of training.
Shortly after, it starts declining, which is loosely correlated with the method's plateau.

\subsection{Synthetic Trace Generation}
\label{sec:synth_trace_analysis}

In resource-constrained environments, available VRAM imposes a strict limitation on the length of distilled reasoning chains.
Once we start truncating them, we face a new challenge: which part of the trace contains the most useful signal?
Finally, how exactly do we prompt the teacher model to generate reasoning traces?

We evaluate reasoning traces by:
(a) \emph{truncation type} - which part of the trace we keep when it exceeds the maximum length;
(b) \emph{length} - the optimal training truncation limit (characters) and evaluation thinking budget (tokens);
(c) \emph{generation method} (below).

Generation methods (details in Appendix~\ref{sec:synth_genration_methods_details}):
(1) \emph{Direct reasoning trace} - baseline prompt to just answer the question;
(2) \emph{Direct reasoning trace (correct only)} - same as (1), but we keep only traces with correct answers;
(3) \emph{Explained answer} - we give a correct answer to the model and prompt it to explain it;
(4) \emph{Direct reasoning trace (with explained)} - same as (1), but replace incorrect traces with (3);
(5) \emph{Corrected answer} - same as (1), but for incorrect traces, we give the model correct answers and ask it to update its reasoning.

Figure~\ref{fig:synth_comparison} shows that models have a slight preference for head truncation, medium to short training chains and thinking budgets.
Surprisingly, longer reasoning chains and greater thinking budgets lead to notable performance decreases.
As to the generation methods, we see that augmented reasoning traces lead to elevated performance, while the consistent data leakage results in degradation.
Based on the findings, we use 8,192-character head truncation and \emph{Corrected answer} generation for the pipeline.

\subsection{Sensitivity Study}

To attribute performance gains to either the data mix or the entropy-based score and sampler, we compare the proposed pipeline (\emph{entropy gain}) with:
(1) alternative pipelines where we sample according to \emph{student entropy} and \emph{discounted entropy gain} (see below);
(2) an alternative pipeline where we use a random sampler.

\emph{Discounted entropy gain} is defined as $\max\{H_S(x)- k(t) * H_P(x),0\}, k(t) \in [0, 1]$, where $t$ is the training epoch.
We set $k(t)$ initially to 0.8 and decrease it according to a schedule, hypothesizing that, as the student undergoes training, the static medium model stops being a good aleatoric proxy.

Table \ref{tab:sampler_ablation} shows that, across three models on average, entropy gain provides the best results, confirming the contribution of the aleatoric uncertainty approximation.
At the same time, we notice that random sampling performs exceptionally well (confirming findings of \citet{okanovic2023repeatedrandomsamplingminimizing}) compared to the baseline, which points to the proposed data mix as a significant contributor to the gains.

\section{Conclusion and Discussion}

This paper introduces a recursive offline distillation method rooted in epistemic uncertainty approximation ($57.15 \pm 1.01$ mean accuracy across three students on MMLU-Pro vs $51.61$ for direct distillation) with a much steeper learning curve (baseline score at 15\% of its data budget).
We confirm that both the entropy difference sampler and the data mix contribute to the gains.
Surprisingly, shorter traces and reasoning budgets perform better.
Further exploration of more rigorous epistemic uncertainty estimation and the surprising effect of the small data mix augmentation remains future work.

\section*{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item The method is tested only with small models. We do not know how well it is going to generalize to larger LLMs. Additionally, it might be challenging to find a suitable aleatoric proxy for larger students.
  \item We use a single dataset. We do not know how well it translates to other settings.
  \item Alternative aleatoric approximations, such as ensemble agreement and others, are left unexplored.
  \item We did not test and compare multiple sizes of aleatoric proxies and their impact on performance.
  \item Different compositions of the data mix are not tested due to lack of resources.
  \item We tested only a single schedule for \emph{Discounted Entropy Gain}. Alternatives remain to be explored.
  \item Some low-resource environments might have even more constraints and therefore struggle to fit a suitable aleatoric proxy into VRAM.
\end{itemize}

\section{Single-token entropy}

Single-token entropy (Figure \ref{fig:single_token_entropy_illustration}) measures a model's uncertainty over the first answer token when prompted to answer a question with a single token.
For a question $x$, we apply softmax to the first-token logits to obtain probabilities $p(v\mid x)$ over the model's full vocabulary $V$, and compute normalized Shannon entropy:
\begin{equation}
  H(x) = -\frac{1}{\log |V|}\sum_{v\in V} p(v\mid x)\log p(v\mid x).
\end{equation}
Dividing by $\log |V|$ puts student and proxy entropies on a common $[0,1]$ scale despite differences in vocabulary size: values near zero indicate a concentrated prediction, while values near one indicate a nearly uniform distribution.

\section{Synthetic trace generation methods}
\label{sec:synth_genration_methods_details}

We compare five ways to construct reasoning-and-answer targets for the same multiple-choice questions. Directly generated option letters are checked against ground-truth labels.

\paragraph{Direct reasoning trace.}
The teacher receives the question and options, generates reasoning, and predicts an option without seeing the ground-truth answer. The baseline retains both correct and incorrect responses.

\paragraph{Direct reasoning trace (correct only).}
We retain only baseline responses whose predicted option matches the ground truth, reducing the training set by discarding failed attempts.

\paragraph{Explained answer.}
The teacher receives the question, options, and correct answer. It generates a step-by-step explanation that reads as a fresh solution, allowing alternatives and self-correction while avoiding acknowledgment of the supplied answer. The target pairs this explanation with the ground-truth option.

\paragraph{Direct reasoning trace (with explained).}
We retain correct baseline responses and replace each incorrect response with the explained-answer response for the same question. This preserves question coverage while restricting answer-conditioned generation to failed attempts.

\paragraph{Corrected answer.}
For each incorrect baseline response, the teacher receives the original reasoning, its incorrect option, and the correct option. It continues the trace toward the correct answer, presenting a natural self-correction without restarting or repeating the original text. We append the continuation and use the ground-truth option as the final answer. Correct baseline responses remain intact.

\subsection{Prompt templates}
The placeholders below are filled with the subject, question, and options. Correct-only filtering and replacement with explanations reuse these prompts; they introduce no additional generation prompt.

\paragraph{Direct generation: system prompt.}
Reasoning is collected separately from the teacher's final answer.
\begin{quote}\small
  The following are multiple choice questions about \{subject\}. Choose a correct option letter. Answer with a single symbol. Do not print anything else.
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Explained answer: system prompt.}
\begin{quote}\small
  The following is a multiple choice question about \{subject\}. The correct answer is provided to you, but your task is to produce a detailed chain-of-thought with step-by-step reasoning that reads as if you were solving the question from scratch---including any natural uncertainty, consideration of multiple options, or self-correction. Do not state or imply that the answer was given to you. End your response with Answer: \texttt{<letter>}.
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Corrected answer: system prompt.}
\begin{quote}\small
  The following are multiple choice questions about \{subject\}. You will be shown a question, a partial chain-of-thought that was previously produced for it, the incorrect option that chain-of-thought arrived at, and the correct option letter. Continue the partial chain-of-thought from where it left off so that it naturally arrives at the correct option. Do not restart the reasoning, do not repeat what was already written, and do not acknowledge that the previous attempt was wrong or that the correct answer was given to you. Write the continuation as if you were the same reasoner noticing a mistake or new consideration mid-thought and revising course. In the end, answer with the correct option letter. End your response with Answer: \texttt{<letter>}.
\end{quote}

\paragraph{User prompts.}
All three begin with:
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
  Question: \{question\}\\
  Options:\\
  a. \{option text\}\\
  b. \{option text\}\\
  \ldots
\end{quote}
For explained answers, append \texttt{Correct option:} followed by the ground-truth letter. For corrections, append:
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
  Partial reasoning so far:\\
  \{original reasoning\}\\
  Incorrect option this reasoning led to: \{predicted letter\}\\
  Correct option: \{ground-truth letter\}
\end{quote}

\subsection{Illustrative examples}
For brevity, consider a toy question with two options: ``A rectangle has sides of length 3 and 2. What is its perimeter? a.\ 6; b.\ 10.'' The following illustrative targets show how the methods handle an incorrect attempt.

\paragraph{Direct trace.}
``The perimeter is $3\times2=6$. Answer: a.'' The baseline retains this response; the correct-only variant discards it.

\paragraph{Explained answer.}
Given correct option b, a fresh explanation is: ``There are two sides of each length, so the perimeter is $2(3+2)=10$. Answer: b.'' The with-explained variant substitutes this response for the incorrect direct trace.

\paragraph{Corrected answer.}
Given the original reasoning, incorrect option a, and correct option b, a continuation is: ``That product gives the area. For the perimeter, I need to add all four sides: $3+2+3+2=10$. Answer: b.'' The target retains the original reasoning, appends this continuation, and ends with b.

\section{Synthetic trace truncation and reasoning caps}
\label{sec:trace_truncation_caps}

Training truncation limits the retained teacher reasoning in characters; the reasoning cap limits the student's generated reasoning at evaluation time in tokens. \subsection{Which part of the trace is retained?}
Head truncation keeps a prefix. Middle truncation keeps an initial segment and a final segment, dropping the interior of an overlong trace. Tail truncation keeps only a suffix. At the nominal 24,000-character setting, head retains up to 24,000 characters, middle retains the first 18,000 and last 6,000 of overlong traces, and tail retains the last 24,000. For corrected traces, middle truncation instead reserves up to 1,600 characters for the correction and uses the remaining space for the original prefix.

Figure~\ref{fig:trace_truncation_modes} compares these policies at a fixed 8,192-token evaluation cap. Preferences vary by model: head gives the best observed checkpoint for LLaMA and Qwen, while tail does so for Phi4-mini.

\section{Sensitivity Study Details}
\label{sec:sensitivity_details}

The sampler ablation holds the corrected traces and the per-epoch mix of 1,024 reasoning examples and 256 answer-only examples fixed, varying the selection rule: uniform random sampling or sampling without replacement weighted by student entropy, positive entropy gain, or discounted entropy gain. The discounted score uses $k(t)=0.8\cdot0.01^{t/100}$, where $t$ is the resampling epoch; its proxy contribution falls to 0.008 at epoch 100. It is labelled ``Combined entropy (sampled)'' in the graphs. Table~\ref{tab:sampler_ablation_full} reports final accuracy; Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity_sampler_comparisons} compares the weighted samplers and random sampling.

Figures~\ref{fig:sensitivity_llama}--\ref{fig:sensitivity_phi} provide individual accuracy and coverage curves. Lines average available seed replicas. Horizontal axes count reasoning-question selections, excluding the additional answer-only examples: 1,024 per standard epoch, $k$ for a fixed-$k$ run, and 9,626 for full-dataset baselines. Accuracy plots share each model's initial chain-of-thought score across evaluation budgets.

Coverage counts distinct question IDs selected at least once, so repeated epochs and response formats do not increase it.
